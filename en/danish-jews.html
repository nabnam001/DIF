The history of the Danish Jews.

A summary spanning four centuries — from the first Sephardic Jew settling in Glückstadt under Christian IV, through changing rights and restrictions, the rescue of October 1943, and the living Jewish community in Denmark today.

Before 1600 — absence

In 1569 Frederik II issued an ordinance on foreigners in Denmark which contained articles on religion (at that time religious freedom did not exist in the country). The law effectively prevented immigration; foreigners could only obtain a lejdebrev (safe-conduct) by special royal permission.

At that point there were probably no Jews in Denmark. When the first Jews actually arrived is not known with certainty. By European standards, Jews came relatively late to Denmark.

The 1600s — the Sephardim arrive

1606: The first Jew is mentioned in Copenhagen — though he had been baptised in Germany. 1619: The first Jew, the Sephardi Dionis, arrives in Glückstadt, newly founded by Christian IV as a trading city.

1622: Christian IV invites a group of Sephardic Jews to settle in Glückstadt. The king hopes that the wealthy and well-educated Sephardim might help revive a strained Danish economy. In 1628 the Jews of the city are granted free religious and trading rights.

1641: Christian IV grants privileges to Jews already living in Hamburg, Altona and Wandsbek (then under the Danish crown). They are given freedom of religion, the right to a synagogue and a cemetery.

1651: Frederik III issues the first ordinance on Jews in Denmark proper — giving them 14 days to obtain a travel pass or leave the country. 1670: Sephardic privileges are confirmed at Christian V's accession. Later Jews are allowed to settle in Fredericia, Nakskov, Ribe and Copenhagen on various conditions.

The 1700s — synagogue and citizenship

1684: The first proper Copenhagen synagogue is established in a private home in Læderstræde. 1788: Reforms open up Jewish citizenship and access to crafts previously reserved for Christians.

1814 — the Letter of Freedom

On 29 March 1814 Frederik VI issued the so-called frihedsbrev (Letter of Freedom) to the Jews. It granted them substantially full civil equality with Danish Christians — more than 30 years before religious freedom became universal under the Constitution of 1849.

1819–20 — The Jewish Feud

For two weeks in September 1819 Copenhagen experienced the so-called Jødefejde (Jewish Feud) — violent unrest with antisemitic motives, inspired by the German "Hep Hep" riots. Police intervened and the disturbances faded, but they cast a long shadow.

1849 — the Constitution and religious freedom

The Constitution introduces full religious freedom. Jews in Denmark thereafter have the same rights as anyone else. Through the 1850s and 60s the Jewish community integrated steadily into Danish culture and commercial life. The Caroline School was founded; the Jewish Community (Det Mosaiske Troessamfund) became formally organised.

The 20th century — pogroms and immigration

From the late 19th century into the early 20th, a substantial wave of Ashkenazi Jews arrived from Russia and Poland, fleeing pogroms. They settled primarily in Copenhagen and brought Yiddish, klezmer and a vibrant religious life with them.

1933–43 — Threat and rescue

With the rise of Nazism in the 1930s came a flow of German refugees. The Danish government was reluctant, and only a few hundred received permanent asylum. Denmark was occupied on 9 April 1940. For the first three years the Germans left the Jewish population alone — part of the so-called "cooperation policy".

On 29 August 1943 cooperation collapsed. On the night of 1–2 October the Gestapo launched their action against the Danish Jews. Thanks to the secret warning from Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz and the courage of an ordinary population, more than 7,220 Jews were rescued to Sweden.

After the war

Most Danish Jews returned home from Sweden in May 1945, finding apartments largely as they had left them — neighbours had paid the rent and watered the plants. The rest of the 20th century brought two new waves of Jewish immigration to Denmark: Polish Jews in 1968–70 after the antisemitic campaign in Poland, and Russian Jews from the 1990s.

Today — a living community

The Jewish Community in Denmark (formerly Det Mosaiske Troessamfund) today numbers around 2,000 members. The synagogue at Krystalgade in Copenhagen dates from 1833. The Caroline School, the Rambam Jewish nursing home, the youth movement B'nei Akiva, Chabad Lubavitch Denmark, Shir Hatzafon (the liberal Jewish congregation), the Danish Jewish Museum and several cultural initiatives such as Jewish Culture in Copenhagen together form a small but vibrant Jewish community.
