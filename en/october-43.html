2 October 1943 — 9 April 1945

October 1943 — The rescue of the Danish Jews.

On the night of 1–2 October 1943 the Nazi action against the Danish Jews was to be carried out. It did not go as planned. Within weeks, around 7,220 Jews and 686 non-Jewish spouses had crossed to Sweden — rescued by an ordinary population that decided to act.

The secret warning

On 28 September 1943 the German diplomat Georg Ferdinand Duckwitz leaked details of the planned action to Danish politicians. Within hours the warning had spread further — through the Jewish Community, resistance networks, doctors, priests and ordinary neighbours — to the Jewish population.

When the Gestapo and the Danish police struck on the night of 1–2 October, most homes stood empty. Only 472 people were found and deported to Theresienstadt. The rest were already in hiding or on their way to the coast.

The flight across the Øresund

In the following weeks the Jews were channelled through an improvised network of doctors' offices, hospitals, churches and private homes down to the coasts of Zealand and Falster — and onward across the Øresund to Sweden in fishing boats, row boats and small craft. Many fishermen risked their lives. Some took payment; others did it for free.

Sweden had opened its borders on 2 October 1943 and accepted everyone. The Danish church played a role too: a pastoral letter from the Danish bishops was read aloud in churches across the country, openly protesting the action.

"The Jews are part of the Danish people, and a persecution of them is a persecution of the Danish people." — Pastoral letter from the Danish bishops, October 1943

Theresienstadt and the white buses

The 472 deported Danish Jews went to Theresienstadt in what was then Czechoslovakia. The Danish government and the Danish Red Cross maintained contact and demanded inspections — one reason why most of the deported Danish Jews survived. Fifty-two perished in the camp.

In March–April 1945 Count Folke Bernadotte and the Swedish Red Cross brought more than 15,000 Scandinavian prisoners out of the German camps in the famous white buses — among them the Danish Jews from Theresienstadt.

The return

On 9 April 1945 the journey home began. The Danish Jews returned to apartments that, in many cases, stood as they had been left — neighbours had cared for them, paid the rent, watered the plants. It is a historical rarity, and one of the reasons the Danish-Jewish history stands out so distinctly from that of many other countries.

Yad Vashem and the Righteous Among the Nations

The Israeli Holocaust memorial Yad Vashem in Jerusalem honours non-Jewish individuals who saved Jews during the Holocaust with the title "The Righteous Among the Nations". The Danish people is the only people honoured collectively on the list — only a handful of individual rescuers are named, but the country itself is recognised as a whole.

International Holocaust Remembrance Day — 27 January — is marked each year in Denmark with lectures and commemorations, including at 27-1.dk. DIF Aarhus participates and supports the observance.

Why we remember

We remember because it is the only way to prevent this from happening again. And because the story of October 1943 is a story about ordinary people who chose to act. It is a heritage the Danish population can be proud of — and a measure to hold the Danish-Israeli friendship up against.
